\subsection*{Part (a)}
\textbf{Perform dimensional analysis following the steps presented in class to determine the non-dimensional relation between nondimensional pressure rise across the pump and the other variables. You can assume that the viscosity of the liquid can be neglected.}

Let $\Delta P = P_2 - P_1$ be the pressure rise across the pump. Neglecting viscosity, we have:
\begin{equation*}
    \Delta P = f(\rho, Q, \Omega, d)
\end{equation*}
The fundamental dimensions of the variables are:
\begin{equation*}
    \begin{array}{c|ccccc}
        & \Delta P & \rho & Q & \Omega & d \\
        \hline
        M & 1 & 1 & 0 & 0 & 0 \\
        L & -1 & -3 & 3 & 0 & 1 \\
        \tau & -2 & 0 & -1 & -1 & 0
    \end{array}
\end{equation*}

With $N = 5$ variables and 3 fundamental dimensions ($M, L, \tau$), the dimensional matrix has rank $R = 3$, giving $N - R = 2$ dimensionless groups. Choosing $\rho, \Omega, d$ as repeating variables:

For $\Pi_1$, containing $\Delta P$:
\begin{align*}
    \Pi_1 &= \Delta P\, \rho^a \Omega^b d^c \\
    M^0 L^0 \tau^0 &= (M L^{-1} \tau^{-2})(M L^{-3})^a (\tau^{-1})^b (L)^c
\end{align*}
Matching exponents:
\begin{align*}
    M:&\ 1 + a = 0 \Rightarrow a = -1 \\
    L:&\ -1 - 3a + c = 0 \Rightarrow c = -2 \\
    \tau:&\ -2 - b = 0 \Rightarrow b = -2
\end{align*}
\begin{equation}
    \Pi_1 = \Delta P\, \rho^{-1} \Omega^{-2} d^{-2} = \frac{\Delta P}{\rho \Omega^2 d^2}
\end{equation}

For $\Pi_2$, containing $Q$:
\begin{align*}
    \Pi_2 &= Q\, \rho^a \Omega^b d^c \\
    M:&\ a = 0 \\
    L:&\ 3 - 3a + c = 0 \Rightarrow c = -3 \\
    \tau:&\ -1 - b = 0 \Rightarrow b = -1
\end{align*}
\begin{equation}
    \Pi_2 = Q\, \Omega^{-1} d^{-3} = \frac{Q}{\Omega d^3}
\end{equation}

By the Buckingham Pi theorem, $\Pi_1 = f(\Pi_2)$:
\begin{equation}
    \frac{\Delta P}{\rho \Omega^2 d^2} = f\left(\frac{Q}{\Omega d^3}\right)
\end{equation}
Defining the pump head as $H \equiv \Delta P / (\rho g)$, this becomes
\begin{equation}
    \boxed{
    \frac{gH}{\Omega^2 d^2} = f\left(\frac{Q}{\Omega d^3}\right)
    }
\end{equation}
i.e. the head coefficient $\psi = gH/(\Omega^2 d^2)$ is a function of the flow coefficient 
$\phi = Q/(\Omega d^3)$. \qed

\subsection*{Part (b)}
\textbf{Plot the data in the table above in non-dimensional form using the non-dimensional variables determined in your analysis.}

The flow rate and head data at each of the three pump speeds (1000, 1475, and 1990 rpm) 
were converted to SI units (gpm $\to$ m$^3$/s, ft $\to$ m, rpm $\to$ rad/s) and combined with 
$g = 9.81 \text{ m/s}^2$ to compute $\phi = Q/(\Omega d^3)$ and $\psi = gH/(\Omega^2 d^2)$. 
The impeller diameter $d$ is not given in the problem; since the same physical pump 
($d = \text{const}$) is used at all three speeds, an arbitrary but consistent value of $d$ 
only rescales both axes uniformly and does not affect whether the three speed curves 
collapse onto a single curve, so $d = 1$ was used as a placeholder value. The resulting 
nondimensional performance curves are shown in Figure~\ref{fig:pump_nondim}

\subsection*{Part (c)}
\textbf{Does your analysis appear to have identified the correct non-dimensional relationship? Why?}

Largely, yes. The three speed curves converge closely onto a single curve 
$\psi = f(\phi)$ across most of the flow range, namely at moderate-to-high flow coefficients, 
which means that $\phi$ and $\psi$ are the dominant governing nondimensional groups for this 
pump. There is a small systematic spread between the curves near shutoff (small $\phi$), where 
$\psi$ decreases slightly with increasing speed rather than collapsing exactly. This residual is 
expected because part (a) neglected viscosity; whereas in the real physical pump, viscous and 
internal losses (captured by a Reynolds number $\text{Re} = \rho \Omega d^2/\mu$ which was 
assumed to be sufficiently negligible) are not perfectly similar across different speeds, since 
Reynolds number increases with $\Omega$. So our inviscid analysis captures the correct 
primary relationship. \qed

\section*{Problem 2}
\textbf{A gas of non-interacting particles of mass $m$ at temperature $T$ has density $\rho$, and internal energy per unit volume $\epsilon$.}

\subsection*{Part (a)}
\textbf{Using dimensional analysis, determine how $\epsilon$ must depend on $\rho, T,$ and $m$. In your formulation use $k_B =$ Boltzmann's constant, $h =$ Planck's constant, and $c =$ speed of light to include possible quantum and relativistic effects.}

We assume $\epsilon = f(\rho, T, m, k_B, h, c)$. The fundamental dimensions of the variables are:
\begin{equation*}
    \begin{array}{c|ccccccc}
        & \epsilon & \rho & T & m & k_B & h & c \\
        \hline
        M & 1 & 1 & 0 & 1 & 1 & 1 & 0 \\
        L & -1 & -3 & 0 & 0 & 2 & 2 & 1 \\
        \tau & -2 & 0 & 0 & 0 & -2 & -1 & -1 \\
        T & 0 & 0 & 1 & 0 & -1 & 0 & 0
    \end{array}
\end{equation*}

With $N = 7$ variables and 4 fundamental dimensions ($M, L, \tau, T$), the dimensional matrix has rank $R = 4$, giving $N - R = 3$ dimensionless groups. Choosing $m, k_B, h,$ and $c$ as repeating variables:

For $\Pi_1$, containing $\epsilon$:
\begin{align*}
    \Pi_1 &= \epsilon\, m^a k_B^b h^c c^d \\
    M^0 L^0 \tau^0 T^0 &= (M L^{-1} \tau^{-2})(M)^a (M L^2 \tau^{-2} T^{-1})^b (M L^2 \tau^{-1})^c (L \tau^{-1})^d
\end{align*}
Matching exponents:
\begin{align*}
    M:&\ 1 + a + b + c = 0 \\
    L:&\ -1 + 2b + 2c + d = 0 \\
    \tau:&\ -2 -2b - c - d = 0 \\
    T:&\ -b = 0
\end{align*}
Solving this system gives $a = -4, b = 0, c = 3, d = -5$. Thus:
\begin{equation}
    \Pi_1 = \epsilon\, m^{-4} h^3 c^{-5} = \frac{\epsilon h^3}{m^4 c^5}
\end{equation}

For $\Pi_2$, containing $\rho$, the identical procedure gives $a = -4, b = 0, c = 3, d = -3$:
\begin{equation}
    \Pi_2 = \rho\, m^{-4} h^3 c^{-3} = \frac{\rho h^3}{m^4 c^3}
\end{equation}

For $\Pi_3$, containing $T$, the identical procedure gives $a = -1, b = 1, c = 0, d = -2$:
\begin{equation}
    \Pi_3 = T\, m^{-1} k_B\, c^{-2} = \frac{k_B T}{m c^2}
\end{equation}

By the Buckingham Pi Theorem, $\Pi_1 = f(\Pi_2, \Pi_3)$. Rearranging for $\epsilon$ gives:
\begin{equation}
    \boxed{
    \epsilon = \frac{m^4 c^5}{h^3} f\left(\frac{\rho h^3}{m^4 c^3}, \frac{k_B T}{m c^2}\right)
    }
\end{equation}

\subsection*{Part (b)}
\textbf{Consider the limit of slow moving particles without quantum effects by requiring $c$ and $h$ to drop out of your dimensionless formulation. How does $\epsilon$ depend on $\rho$ and $T$? What type of gas follows this thermodynamic law?}

Assume a power-law form for the arbitrary function, $f(\Pi_2, \Pi_3) = \gamma\, (\Pi_2)^\alpha (\Pi_3)^\beta$, where $\gamma \in \mathbb{R}$. Expanding this into the relation for $\epsilon$:
\begin{equation*}
    \epsilon = \gamma\, \frac{m^4 c^5}{h^3}\left(\frac{\rho h^3}{m^4 c^3}\right)^\alpha \left(\frac{k_B T}{m c^2}\right)^\beta = \gamma\, \rho^\alpha T^\beta k_B^\beta\, m^{(4 - 4\alpha - \beta)} h^{(3\alpha - 3)} c^{(5 - 3\alpha - 2\beta)}
\end{equation*}
To ensure $h$ and $c$ drop out, their exponents must equal zero:
\begin{align*}
    h:&\ 3\alpha - 3 = 0 \Rightarrow \alpha = 1 \\
    c:&\ 5 - 3\alpha - 2\beta = 0 \Rightarrow \beta = 1
\end{align*}
Substituting $\alpha = 1$ and $\beta = 1$ back into the expression gives:
\begin{equation}
    \boxed{
    \epsilon = \gamma\, \frac{\rho k_B T}{m}
    }
\end{equation}
The internal energy per unit volume depends linearly on density and temperature. This follows 
the thermodynamic law for an \textbf{ideal gas} (ideal gas law). \qed

\subsection*{Part (c)}
\textbf{Consider the limit of massless particles (i.e. photons) by requiring $m$ and $\rho$ to drop out of your dimensionless formulation of part a). How does $\epsilon$ depend on $T$ in this case? What is the name of this radiation law?}

By the expanded power-law equation, we require the exponents of $m$ and $\rho$ to be zero:
\begin{align*}
    \rho:&\ \alpha = 0 \\
    m:&\ 4 - 4\alpha - \beta = 0 \Rightarrow \beta = 4
\end{align*}
Substituting $\alpha = 0$ and $\beta = 4$ back into the expression:
\begin{equation}
    \boxed{
    \epsilon = \gamma\, \frac{k_B^4 T^4}{h^3 c^3}
    }
\end{equation}
The internal energy per unit volume is proportional to $T^4$. This is the 
\textbf{Stefan-Boltzmann Law}. \qed

\section*{Problem 3}
\textbf{The vertical motion of a ship in water is governed by the $z$-component of the Navier--Stokes equation:}
\begin{equation*}
    \pd{w}{t} + u\pd{w}{x} + v\pd{w}{y} + w\pd{w}{z} = -\frac{1}{\rho}\pd{p}{z} - g + \frac{\mu}{\rho}\left(\frac{\partial^2 w}{\partial x^2} + \frac{\partial^2 w}{\partial y^2} + \frac{\partial^2 w}{\partial z^2}\right)
\end{equation*}
\textbf{where $u, v, w$ are the $x$, $y$, and $z$ velocities respectively, and all other variables have their normal meanings. The flow velocity far from the ship is given by $U$ and the length of the ship at the waterline is $l$.}

\subsection*{Part (a)}
\textbf{Non-dimensionalize the governing equation, the $z$-component of Navier--Stokes.}

We define characteristic scales to normalize the variables:
\begin{equation*}
    x^* = \frac{x}{l}, \quad y^* = \frac{y}{l}, \quad z^* = \frac{z}{l}, \quad u^* = \frac{u}{U}, \quad v^* = \frac{v}{U}, \quad w^* = \frac{w}{U}, \quad t^* = \frac{tU}{l}, \quad p^* = \frac{p}{\rho U^2}
\end{equation*}
Substituting these scales into each term of the governing equation (with $\nu = \mu/\rho$ the kinematic viscosity):
\begin{align*}
    \partial_t w &= \frac{U^2}{l}\,\partial_{t^*} w^*, &
    u\,\partial_x w &= \frac{U^2}{l}\,u^*\,\partial_{x^*} w^*, \\
    v\,\partial_y w &= \frac{U^2}{l}\,v^*\,\partial_{y^*} w^*, &
    w\,\partial_z w &= \frac{U^2}{l}\,w^*\,\partial_{z^*} w^*, \\
    \frac{1}{\rho}\,\partial_z p &= \frac{U^2}{l}\,\partial_{z^*} p^*, &
    \frac{\mu}{\rho}\nabla^2 w &= \frac{\nu U}{l^2}\nabla^{*2} w^*
\end{align*}
Substituting these into the governing equation gives:
\begin{equation*}
    \frac{U^2}{l}\left(\partial_{t^*} w^* + u^*\partial_{x^*} w^* + v^*\partial_{y^*} w^* + w^*\partial_{z^*} w^*\right) = -\frac{U^2}{l}\partial_{z^*} p^* - g + \frac{\nu U}{l^2}\nabla^{*2}w^*
\end{equation*}
Dividing through by the convective acceleration term ($U^2/l$) gives:
\begin{equation}
    \boxed{
    \partial_{t^*} w^* + u^*\partial_{x^*} w^* + v^*\partial_{y^*} w^* + w^*\partial_{z^*} w^* = -\partial_{z^*} p^* - \frac{g l}{U^2} + \frac{\mu}{\rho U l} \nabla^{*2} w^*
    }
\end{equation}

\subsection*{Part (b)}
\textbf{What common non-dimensional parameters result?}

The resulting common non-dimensional parameters are:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Inverse Froude Number squared: $\dfrac{1}{Fr^2} = \dfrac{g l}{U^2}$
    \item Inverse Reynolds Number: $\dfrac{1}{Re} = \dfrac{\mu}{\rho U l}$
\end{itemize}

\subsection*{Part (c)}
\textbf{Assume you didn't have knowledge of the governing equation but knew that the $z$-velocity, $w$, was a function of $\rho, g, \mu, U,$ and $l$. Find the non-dimensional parameters using the Buckingham PI theorem.}

We assume $w = f(\rho, g, \mu, U, l)$. The fundamental dimensions of the variables are:
\begin{equation*}
    \begin{array}{c|cccccc}
        & w & \rho & g & \mu & U & l \\
        \hline
        M & 0 & 1 & 0 & 1 & 0 & 0 \\
        L & 1 & -3 & 1 & -1 & 1 & 1 \\
        \tau & -1 & 0 & -2 & -1 & -1 & 0
    \end{array}
\end{equation*}
With 6 variables ($w, \rho, g, \mu, U, l$) and 3 fundamental dimensions ($M, L, \tau$), we choose $\rho, U, l$ as repeating variables to form $6 - 3 = 3$ dimensionless groups.

For $\Pi_1$, containing $w$:
\begin{align*}
    \Pi_1 &= w\, \rho^a U^b l^c \\
    M:&\ a = 0, \quad L:\ 1 - 3a + b + c = 0, \quad \tau:\ -1 - b = 0 \Rightarrow a = 0,\ b = -1,\ c = 0
\end{align*}
\begin{equation}
    \Pi_1 = \frac{w}{U}
\end{equation}

For $\Pi_2$, containing $g$: solving analogously gives $a = 0,\ b = -2,\ c = 1$:
\begin{equation}
    \Pi_2 = \frac{g l}{U^2} \quad \text{(Inverse Froude Number squared)}
\end{equation}

For $\Pi_3$, containing $\mu$: solving analogously gives $a = -1,\ b = -1,\ c = -1$:
\begin{equation}
    \Pi_3 = \frac{\mu}{\rho U l} \quad \text{(Inverse Reynolds Number)}
\end{equation}

By the Buckingham Pi theorem, $\Pi_1 = f(\Pi_2, \Pi_3)$, i.e.
\begin{equation}
    \boxed{
    \frac{w}{U} = f(Fr, Re) = f\left(\frac{U}{\sqrt{gl}}, \frac{\rho U l}{\mu}\right)
    }
\end{equation}
This matches the parameters found directly from the governing equation in part (b), as expected: pressure was not included among the given variables here, so no Euler-number-like group appears. \qed
\subsection*{Part (d)}
\textbf{We wish to test a scaled model of a tanker ship at scale of 1/100 the actual size, is it practical to achieve dynamic similarity?}

Let subscripts $m$ and $p$ denote the model and prototype (full-scale), respectively, with $l_m/l_p = 1/100$. Dynamic similarity requires matching both the Froude number and the Reynolds number:
\begin{equation*}
    Fr_m = Fr_p \Rightarrow \frac{U_m}{\sqrt{g l_m}} = \frac{U_p}{\sqrt{g l_p}} \Rightarrow \frac{U_m}{U_p} = \sqrt{\frac{l_m}{l_p}} = \frac{1}{10}
\end{equation*}
\begin{equation*}
    Re_m = Re_p \Rightarrow \frac{U_m l_m}{\nu_m} = \frac{U_p l_p}{\nu_p} \Rightarrow \frac{\nu_m}{\nu_p} = \left(\frac{U_m}{U_p}\right)\left(\frac{l_m}{l_p}\right) = \left(\frac{1}{10}\right)\left(\frac{1}{100}\right) = \frac{1}{1000}
\end{equation*}
So Froude scaling requires the model velocity to be $1/10$ of the prototype velocity, and 
simultaneously satisfying Reynolds scaling in this case requires a model fluid with kinematic 
viscosity $1/1000$ that of the prototype fluid. Taking 
$\nu_p \approx 1\times10^{-6}\ \text{m}^2/\text{s}$ (water at $20^\circ$C) gives 
$\nu_m \approx 1\times10^{-9}\ \text{m}^2/\text{s}$, roughly $1000\times$ less viscous than 
water. No practical fluid has a kinematic viscosity anywhere near this low.

It is therefore \textbf{not practical} to achieve full dynamic similarity: matching the Froude 
number and the Reynolds number simultaneously would require a model test fluid whose kinematic 
viscosity is unrealistically small, so the two scaling requirements cannot both be satisfied 
with any real fluid. \qed
